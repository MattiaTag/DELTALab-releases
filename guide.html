DELTALab — User Guide

Who this is for: geochemists processing clumped and bulk stable-isotope data (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, Δ₄₇, Δ₄₈).

App version: 1.0.3 · Guide updated: 2026-11-13

DELTALab turns raw mass-spectrometer output into standardised Δ values and temperatures. It corrects the background, puts your data on a reference frame and converts Δ to temperature. You get replicate and sample tables, diagnostic plots and exports you can use in a paper. The clumped-isotope maths gives the same numbers as D47crunch (Daëron 2021) and D47calib (Daëron & Vermeesch 2024).

New to DELTALab? Read chapter 1 (the seven key words). Come back to the other chapters when you need them.

How this guide is written:

  • Button and menu names are in bold, exactly as they appear in the app.
  • "→" means "then click". For example, Contacts → Learning Routine.
  • Every chapter stands on its own, so you can open any one of them directly.

1. Key words

DELTALab uses seven words everywhere.

Acquisition — One block of measurement cycles from the instrument. Each cycle records the sample and reference-gas beams at m/z 44–49 (plus the 47.5 half-mass, if your instrument has that cup). An acquisition is the smallest unit you import.

Replicate — One analysis of one sample. DELTALab joins back-to-back acquisitions of the same sample into one replicate. Standardisation, exclusion and temperature all work on replicates.

Sample — The material you analysed, known to DELTALab by its sample name. Replicates with exactly the same sample name are one sample, also when they are in different sessions. A sample is either a standard or an unknown, a sample you want a result for. In the app, Samples on its own (the summary bar, Filter to) means the unknowns only.

Session — A batch of replicates that you process together, usually one instrument run. A session has its own background correction, standardisation fit, reference gas, acid reaction temperature and mass spectrometer. Almost every screen works on a session or a group of sessions.

Standard (also: reference material) — A sample with an accepted value, such as ETH-1 to ETH-4, IAEA-C1/C2, equilibrated gases or an in-house carbonate. Their values live in Settings → Reference material (chapter 7). A standard you use to constrain a fit is simply called a standard here.

Standardisation — Moving your raw Δ values (Δ₄₇ raw) onto a community reference frame (Δ₄₇, for example I-CDES). DELTALab does this by fitting a transfer function through your standards. The fit can also follow a slow drift over time. Bulk Standardization does the same for δ¹³C and δ¹⁸O.

Isotope system — What you are measuring: Clumped Isotopes (Δ₄₇) or Dual Clumped (Δ₄₇ and Δ₄₈). You choose it in the menu of the top bar (chapter 2). It decides which tabs you see and which Δ you process.

Raw values are kept apart from results

The voltages of every cycle are stored at import and never changed. DELTALab calculates the raw values (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, Δ₄₇ raw, Δ₄₈ raw) from them.

Standardisation, temperature conversion and the δ¹⁸O acid correction never overwrite a raw value. Each writes to its own columns, so you can run them again as often as you like without losing data.

A raw value changes only when DELTALab calculates it again from the cycles:

  • after a Negative Background Correction (chapter 5);
  • when you exclude cycles or an acquisition (chapter 4.1);
  • when you change a session's reference gas (chapter 6).

Moving, renaming or deleting replicates does not change the raw value of any other replicate.

Δ₄₇ raw and the standardised Δ₄₇ are separate columns. δ¹³C and δ¹⁸O have one column each in the Replicate list: it shows the standardised value once Bulk Standardization has run, and "—" until then. Choose raw in the column's header menu to see the raw value, or hover over a standardised δ¹³C or δ¹⁸O to see the raw value behind it.

The red flag: "standardisation is out of date"

A red flag at the right end of a session's row means its standardisation is out of date. In the picture above, "Demo run 2" has one. Something changed after the last standardisation, for example:

  • you excluded or included a replicate;
  • you imported more data into the session;
  • you moved, renamed or deleted replicates in the Sessions Editor (chapter 6).

The old values stay on screen until you re-run standardisation (chapter 5). Then the flag disappears.

The flag also disappears if you simply undo the change, for example by including again exactly the replicates you had excluded.

A new reference gas or a split works differently: it clears the session's standardised values, so there is no flag. Δ₄₇ shows "—" until you standardise again.

A session with no flag is either up to date or not standardised yet.

The steps for Qtegra and Isodat files

  1. Choose the tab (1).
  2. Drop your files on the drop area (2), or click Browse (3). The drop area then says how many files are waiting.
  3. Choose the session the data goes into (4).
  4. For a new session, choose its mass spectrometer (5).
  5. Click Import (6). It stays greyed out until at least one file is waiting.

When the import is done the session is in the session list. If everything in your files was imported, the window closes. If something was left out, the window stays open and tells you what and why (see "When DELTALab leaves something out" below).

Good to know:

  • You can add files in several goes. Dropping or browsing again adds to the files that are waiting. A file that is already waiting is not added twice.
  • Remove all, in the drop area, forgets the waiting files so you can start again.
  • CSV files and .did files cannot wait together. If CSV files are waiting and you choose .did files (or the other way round), the new files replace the old ones.
  • If something is missing when you click Import, there is no message. The field you still have to fill, the session name or the mass spectrometer, gets a red outline.
  • Cancel, or a click outside the window, closes it and forgets the waiting files.

Qtegra (CSV files)

Choose one or more Qtegra CSV exports. Several files are fine: they are all added to the same session.

  • DELTALab detects whether the file uses commas or semicolons.
  • DELTALab works out whether the dates are written day-first or month-first. It decides once for all the files you import together, and then checks every row.
  • If the dates of a file could be read either way, that file is refused rather than risk putting your replicates in the wrong order. A wrong date would silently break both the replicate order and the drift correction. The window names the file and says why. To fix it, export again with an unambiguous date format (for example YYYY-MM-DD).
  • Rows with a timestamp DELTALab cannot read are skipped. The window tells you how many rows, and in which file.
  • If the same acquisition is in two of your files, it is imported once. The window tells you.

Isodat (.did files)

Choose all the .did files of a run (one acquisition each). DELTALab reads the Thermo files directly, so you don't need to export them from Isodat first. A file DELTALab cannot read is skipped, and the window tells you which file and why.

DELTALab then groups the files into samples. This is called binning. Choose how next to Browse:

  • Automated binning (the default): each file's sample name is read from the Identifier 1 field that the operator typed in Isodat. If that field is empty, DELTALab uses the start of the file name instead.
  • Manual binning: a second panel opens on the right, where you give the sample names yourself.

How to bin by hand:

  1. Tick the files that belong to one sample.
  2. Type the sample name in the box at the bottom.
  3. Click Add group. The group appears at the top with its number of files.
  4. Repeat until the panel says "All files assigned."

The × on a group removes it, and its files go back to the list. Import stays greyed out until every file is in a group.

Easotope database

Use this tab to bring data over from an Easotope database.

Before you start:

  • Close Easotope. An open Easotope locks the database, and DELTALab then says so.
  • You do not need Easotope or Java installed. DELTALab brings its own.

Steps:

  1. Drop the Easotope .h2.db file, or the folder that holds it, on the drop area. Or click Browse and choose the file. After an import, DELTALab remembers the file for next time.
  2. Click Analyze. This is a safe trial run: nothing is written yet. A second panel opens on the right and lists every session DELTALab found.
  3. For each session, check the name, the isotope system and the mass spectrometer. Change them if needed. Untick any session you don't want.
  4. Click Import in the right-hand panel. DELTALab takes a safety backup first (chapter 8), then brings in the ticked sessions.

If Analyze shows a message in place of the list:

  • "the Easotope database is locked — close Easotope and retry": Easotope is still open. Close it and click Analyze again.
  • "no *.h2.db file found in …": the folder you chose holds no Easotope database.
  • "found more than one *.h2.db file in …": choose the database file itself, not the folder.
  • "Found 0 sessions": the database holds no replicate that is inside a correction interval.

How DELTALab builds the sessions:

  • Each Easotope correction interval becomes one session.
  • An interval is split wherever the reference gas changes. The second part gets "(b)" after its name, the third "(c)".
  • A replicate that is in no correction interval is not imported. The panel says how many there are.

What each session's line tells you:

  • the number of replicates and scans;
  • how many replicates are already imported, if any. They are skipped, so importing the same database again is safe;
  • the acid temperature the session will get. "(from …)" or "(built-in default)" means the value does not come from Easotope.
  • A line in bold is a warning. Read it before you import.

What comes across:

  • Only raw acquisitions, reference gas and scans are imported. Easotope's own corrected or standardised values are not. DELTALab recalculates everything itself.
  • Every replicate in a correction interval is imported. Replicates that were disabled in Easotope arrive excluded, and so do disabled cycles (chapter 4.1).
  • Reference gas: the values recorded in Easotope for that session.
  • Acid temperature: Easotope's value when all replicates of the session agree. Otherwise the session gets the mass spectrometer's value, or 90 °C for an instrument DELTALab does not know yet. If the replicates differ, a warning tells you.
  • Mass spectrometer: the one recorded in Easotope is preselected. Every session needs one; a session without one gets a red outline when you click Import. An instrument DELTALab has not seen before is added to Settings → Mass spectrometers (chapter 7) with that session's reference gas and acid temperature.

Choosing the session and the mass spectrometer

On the Qtegra and Isodat tabs, choose where the data goes (4 in the first picture):

  • New session: DELTALab suggests a name from the first file. Type your own if you prefer.
  • Existing session: the new acquisitions are added to that session. If the suggested name is already a session, DELTALab chooses it for you. Deactivated sessions are not in this list.

Every session must have a mass spectrometer. The Mass spec line (5) is shown when the session has none yet: for a new session, or for an old session imported before mass spectrometers existed.

  • Pick the instrument from the menu. If you have only one, it is already chosen.
  • The session gets the reference gas and the acid temperature of that instrument, and keeps its own copy of these values. Change them later in the Sessions Editor (chapter 6).
  • Another instrument is not added here: the line carries a hint, and new instruments are added in Settings → Mass spectrometers (chapter 7).

Importing the same data twice is safe

DELTALab recognises an acquisition by its date and time. One that is already in the database, in any session, is skipped. So you can import a folder again after adding new files to it: only the new acquisitions come in. The window tells you how many were skipped.

When DELTALab leaves something out

DELTALab never leaves data out silently. This applies to the Qtegra and Isodat tabs.

Some data was imported, some was not. The window stays open. It says how many replicates were imported and into which session, and lists under Not imported: everything that was left out, with the reason. Click Close when you have read it.

Nothing was imported. The window stays open with your files still waiting. In red it says "No new, readable acquisitions found.", and below that it gives the reason for each file. Choose Remove all and other files, or Cancel.

The reasons you can see:

The window says What it means
"… already imported" These acquisitions (Qtegra) or files (Isodat) are already in the database, in this or another session.
"… that another file of this import already holds" The same acquisition is in two of your files. It was imported once.
"dropped … row(s) with an unreadable timestamp" These rows of a Qtegra file have no date DELTALab can read.
"cannot tell whether timestamps … are DD/MM or MM/DD" The dates of this Qtegra file can be read both ways. The file is refused (see above).
"skipped — this batch is being read as …" This file's dates contradict the other files you chose with it.
"doesn't look like a Qtegra export" The file is not a Qtegra CSV.
"could not be read" The CSV file is damaged or empty.
"Skipped … .did: …" DELTALab cannot read this .did file, or it has no sample cycles.
"… sample cycle(s) have no matching reference cycle and were dropped" The acquisition stopped early. The complete cycles were imported.

The same list is kept in Session History (chapter 4.3), under the import's own line, so you can read it again after you close the window.

Adding data to a session that is already standardised

Important:

the new replicates get a provisional Δ₄₇ straight away, using the session's existing fit. Their uncertainties stay blank and the session gets a red flag. Re-run standardisation (chapter 5) to update everything properly. A session standardised with the Easotope Moving window has no session fit, so its new replicates stay blank until you run it again.

Merging another DELTALab database

Open Import and choose the DELTALab tab. Browse to the other .db file and press Analyze: every session in that file is listed with what merging would do.

Label Meaning
New The session is not in your database. It is copied as it is, with its settings and history.
Add N A session with that name exists. Only the N replicates you do not have yet are added, and they are recalculated with your session's settings.
Already here Every replicate is already in your database. Nothing to do.

A replicate counts as already there when its acquisition time matches one in your database, whichever session holds it. You can therefore merge the same file again later and only new replicates are added. If a session of the same name was measured on another instrument, the incoming one is added as "name (merged)". The other file is only read, and a backup of your database is taken first. After adding replicates to an existing session, re-run standardisation (chapter 5).

3.1 How to setup QTegra Export

DELTALab reads the raw beam intensities of a Qtegra export and works out the deltas itself (chapter 3, "Qtegra (CSV files)"). So the export must hold the intensities and a few identifying columns. You set this up once in Qtegra's Query page and save it as a preset, and every later export is two clicks.

  1. Open the Labbook you want to export and click Query in the list on the left.
  2. Under the tab called "Categories", select only Standard Intensity and Sample Intensity.
  3. In the Columns section:
    1. Under Sample List, tick Start Time, Category and Label.
    2. Under Results, tick Trace Results.
  4. Click the reload button (the circle made of two arrows) to load the data with the structure you defined. Then click the weight button, next to the reload button, to remove the measuring unit from the table. The table should now have one row per cycle: the category, the start time, the label (your sample name), and one intensity per mass.
  5. Presets. When the table looks like the one in the picture, save it under a name (here Intensities_Only) with the save button next to the box. Next time, choose the preset and the categories and columns are set for you.

  6. Click Export data in the toolbar. The export window opens.

  7. Set Exporter to Query CSV Export.

  8. Under CSV Export Options, choose the Path (the folder) and the Filename. Set the Decimal Symbol to a point (.): DELTALab does not read numbers written with a decimal comma.
  9. Click Export.

The Explore tab: Δ against time

One point per replicate, with Δ₄₇ on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Use it to see a whole run at a glance: drift, jumps, outliers, and how well the standards agree with their accepted values.

The value buttons (left of the plot, 4 in the picture):

  • Measured: Δ₄₇ raw. The plot opens on this view.
  • Corrected: the standardised Δ₄₇.
  • Residuals: how far each point sits from the standardisation fit, around a zero line. Use it to judge the fit and to spot a drifting session.

Corrected and Residuals need a standardisation. Until you have run one, every point sits on a flat line at zero. After a run, Go to Explore on the Standardization page opens this plot directly on Corrected.

The button above the plot names the Δ you are looking at. On Dual Clumped there are two, Δ₄₇ and Δ₄₈, and they switch between the masses.

The time-axis buttons (under the plot, 5 in the picture):

  • Date & Time: real dates, with gaps where nothing was measured.
  • Time Normalized: measurement order, with equal spacing. Thin lines mark where one session ends and the next begins.

Reading the plot:

  • Standards are filled, coloured markers. Each standard has its own colour and shape, set in the Marker column of Settings → Reference material (chapter 7).
  • Unknown samples are open grey circles.
  • Excluded replicates stay on the plot, pale grey, so you can still see where they were.
  • Coloured horizontal lines mark each standard's accepted Δ, with its name at the left end. A line is only drawn when its value falls inside the range of the plot.
    • In Corrected, each standard should scatter around its own line.
    • In Measured, the points do not sit on the lines. Raw values are not on the reference frame yet, so most lines fall outside the plot.
    • In Residuals, there is one zero line instead.
  • Hover over a point to see its sample, session, ID, date and value.

The size of the points is set by Marker size in Settings → Display preferences.

Important:

a standard only counts as a standard on this page if its Show in plots box is ticked for the isotope system you are in (Settings → Reference material). With the box unticked it is drawn, counted and filtered like an unknown sample. This has no effect on which standards a fit uses.

The Summary tab

One row per sample, in alphabetical order. Excluded replicates are left out; n is the number of replicates that remain.

  • δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O: the sample averages. A δ¹⁸O AF column appears after δ¹⁸O as soon as any replicate in view has an acid-fractionation-corrected value (chapter 5.1).
  • Δ₄₇: the sample's standardised value, calculated the same way as D47crunch's sample table. It is not just the average of the replicates. Before standardisation, it shows the average instead.
  • SE: standard error, including both the measurement and the standardisation uncertainty.
  • 95 % CL: 95 % confidence limit (SE × Student's t).
  • SD: how much the sample's replicates scatter (standard deviation, n − 1).
  • T, T SE: the average temperature and the average temperature uncertainty.

Dual Clumped adds Δ₄₈ with its own SE, 95 % CL and SD.

A small coloured dot after δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, δ¹⁸O AF, Δ₄₇, Δ₄₈ and T says whether that value can be used as it is: green, yellow, orange or red. Chapter 4.4 explains the colours and what to do about each.

SE and SD are different things. SE is the calculated uncertainty. SD is how much your replicates actually scatter. A sample with a single replicate has no SD ("—"), so SE is its only error bar. Do not calculate SE as SD ÷ √n; that gives a different, wrong number.

The Summary always lists every sample of the selected sessions. It ignores Filter to.

The Replicates tab

One row per replicate. The columns are, from left to right:

  1. QC: a round dot. Click it to exclude or include the replicate (chapter 4.1).
  2. Paperclip: files and a note attached to this replicate (chapter 4.1).
  3. ID
  4. Date & Time
  5. Sample: click it to open the raw cycles in the acquisitions window (chapter 4.1).

  6. δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O: the δ¹⁸O header is a menu that picks which δ¹⁸O the column shows:

    • δ¹⁸O (default): standardised δ¹⁸O. "—" until Bulk Standardization has run.
    • δ¹⁸O raw: the value from import, never standardised.
    • δ¹⁸O AF: the mineral δ¹⁸O from the acid fractionation correction (chapter 5.1). "—" for samples not corrected.

    Your choice stays when you switch tabs or isotope systems.

  7. Δ₄₇ raw. Dual Clumped also has Δ₄₈ raw.

  8. Δ₄₇: standardised Δ₄₇. Dual Clumped also has Δ₄₈.
  9. Δ₄₇ SE: the sample's standard error from standardisation, repeated on each of its replicates.
  10. T (°C), T SE: temperature and its uncertainty.

δ¹³C and δ¹⁸O show the standardised value once Bulk Standardization has run, and the raw value until then. Hover over a standardised value to see the raw value behind it.

A cell shows "—" when there is no value yet:

  • Δ₄₇ and Δ₄₇ SE need standardisation (chapter 5).
  • T (°C) needs temperature conversion.
  • T SE needs temperature conversion with Propagate calibration uncertainties ticked.

The Dual Equilibrium tab (Dual Clumped only)

Plots Δ₄₇ against Δ₄₈ together with the theoretical equilibrium curve. The numbers along the curve are temperatures in °C.

  • Replicates shows one point per replicate.
  • Summary shows one point per sample. Hover over it to see how many replicates it averages.

Samples that plot away from the curve point to kinetic effects or a correction problem.

Filter to decides what is drawn: set it to Samples to hide the standards.

The Negative Background tab

Plots Δ₄₇ (vertical) against δ₄₇ (horizontal) for your standards, with one regression line per standard group and a shaded 95 % confidence band around it. On Dual Clumped the Δ₄₇ and Δ₄₈ buttons switch to the mass-48 pair.

A good background factor makes these lines flat. Use the plot to check the minimizer's result, or to judge a fixed factor by eye (chapter 5).

  • Gas and Carbonates choose which family of groups is shown.
  • Measured and Corrected choose which Δ value is plotted.
  • The groups come from the Neg. Bkg eval. column of Settings → Reference material. If no standard has a group, the plot says "No standard groups defined".

4.4 The traffic light

The traffic light is the small coloured dot after each reported value in the Summary tab (chapter 4). It answers one question: can this value be reported as it is, for the replicates I selected?

  • Point at a dot to see why it has that colour: a headline, the reasons, then notes on how the value was produced.
  • Click a dot to keep its tooltip open, so you can select and copy the text. Click the dot again, click elsewhere or press Esc to close it.
  • Reference entry in the tooltip is a link while the tooltip is kept open. It opens Session History (chapter 4.3) on the entry of the standardisation behind the value.

Why the selection matters

A standardisation writes one value per sample for all the sessions it was run on. The Summary then shows that value for whatever sessions you select. The two do not always match:

  • You standardised sessions A and B together, and select only A. The value of a sample measured in both still contains B's replicates.
  • You standardised A and B separately, and select both. There are two values for the same sample.

The traffic light tells you which case you are in, and whether it is a problem.

This chapter calls each click of Apply on the Standardization page a run. The app's tooltips say "standardization"; the export calls them Standardization runs and numbers them (#4).

Several standardisations behind one Δ value

When the selected replicates of a sample were standardised in separate runs, DELTALab checks whether their values may be combined. They may when all of this is true:

  1. No session is shared between the runs. Each value then comes from its own measurements of the standards, so the values are statistically independent.
  2. The runs used the same standards.
  3. Each standard had the same accepted value in every run.
  4. Each run produced an SE.

If so, the Summary shows the weighted mean of the runs' values. Each value counts in proportion to its precision:

w = 1 / SE²

Δ = Σ(w · Δ) / Σw

SE = 1 / √(Σw)

95 % CL = SE × t, with Student's t for the combined degrees of freedom (Welch–Satterthwaite).

  • Two runs with the same SE give the plain average, with an SE smaller by √2.
  • This is the calculation Independent standardisation does inside one run (chapter 5).
  • The tooltip says "This value is the mean of 2 separate standardizations, each weighted by its error." and names each one.
  • Nothing is stored: the combined value is calculated from the stored values every time, on screen and in the exported Summary alike.

Runs with different settings combine too: different drift terms (Pooled, Independent) or different Easotope ETF options. These are chosen for each session, as in D47crunch, and the tooltip adds a note that says so.

If one of the four conditions fails, the dot is red. The Summary then shows the value of the first run only, so do not use it for the whole selection.

Red: what makes a value not allowable

Δ₄₇ and Δ₄₈: the tooltip lists every problem that applies, one sentence each, in the order of the table, followed by one line per standardisation. A second problem is therefore not hidden behind the first.

What the traffic light does not check

A green dot does not cover these cases. Check them yourself.

  • T does not follow its Δ₄₇. A temperature can be green while the Δ₄₇ it was converted from is orange or red. Look at both dots.
  • A standard's accepted value edited after a single standardisation. The stored values still use the old one. Standardise again after editing a standard (chapter 7).
  • An acid reaction temperature edited after Bulk Standardization. δ¹⁸O still uses the old temperature. Run Bulk Standardization again (chapter 5.1).
  • A change in a partner session. After Pooled on A and B together, a change in B turns only B orange. A sample measured only in A stays green, although a new run would move its value slightly.

Card: Negative Background Correction

Real data has a negative background on m/z 47. DELTALab removes it from every cycle with the m/z 47.5 half-mass beam:

V47 new = V47 − factor × V47.5

The card needs data that was imported with its raw acquisitions. Choose one of three methods and click Apply.

  • Apply fixed scaling factors. Type the mass-47 factor. It is applied to every selected session. Type a positive number. This is different from Easotope.
  • Apply minimizer (Bernecker et al., 2023). DELTALab finds, for each selected session, the factor that makes Δ₄₇ raw flattest against δ⁴⁷ within each group of standards.
    • Tick the Standard groups to use.
    • Groups come from the Neg. Bkg eval. column of Settings → Reference material (chapter 7). Put standards that were heated or equilibrated at the same temperature in the same group.
    • A "Gas" group needs at least one standard; any other group needs at least two. A group with too few is left out.
    • The factor that was found is written in Session History (chapter 4.3).
  • Scan-based correction (Bernasconi et al., 2013). Uses fitted HV scans instead of the half-mass beam. It is meant mainly for instruments without an m/z 47.5 cup.
    • It works on exactly one session at a time. With no session or several selected, the card says "Select exactly one session to manage its scans."
    • The session needs at least one fitted scan set (chapter 6.1). Without one, the card says "No scans for this session."
    • Tick the Scan sets to use, then choose how to Combine sets: Nearest, Average or Interpolate. Choose one even if you ticked a single set.

After Apply, Δ₄₇ raw is recalculated and everything after this card is cleared. Run the cards below again.

Card: Standardization Protocol

Fits the Δ₄₇ raw of your standards to their accepted values, then applies that fit to every replicate.

  1. Tick the Standards. The list shows every reference material from Settings → Reference material (chapter 7), whether or not the selected sessions contain it. This choice has nothing to do with which standards are drawn in the plots.
  2. Choose the protocol.
    • Pooled Session standardization (Daëron, 2021). All selected sessions are fitted together. Each session keeps its own fit, but an unknown measured in several sessions gets one shared Δ₄₇. Use this when your sessions share standards.
    • Independent Session standardization (Daëron, 2021). Each session is fitted on its own, from its own standards. An unknown measured in several sessions is reported as the weighted average of its per-session values.
    • Easotope ETF standardization (John & Bowen, 2016). Easotope's empirical transfer function. Each session is fitted on its own with a straight line from Δ₄₇ raw to the standards' accepted values, with no δ⁴⁷ term and no drift. Each standard replicate is corrected by the line fitted without itself; every other replicate uses the whole-session line.
  3. Allow temporal drift in a / b / c (optional, Pooled and Independent only). Tick a coefficient to let it change steadily over time within the fit. a is the scrambling factor, b the compositional slope and c the reference offset, as named in the result box.
  4. Click Apply.

The Easotope options. Two boxes under the Easotope choice change how its lines are fitted. You can tick one of them or neither.

  • Moving window: each replicate gets its own line, fitted on the N nearest standard replicates before it and the N after it. Type N in the box; it starts at 10. There is no session line, so the result box has no Parameters table, and replicates you import later stay blank until the next run. If a window holds fewer than two different standards, the run is refused and nothing changes.
  • Average standards first: each standard's replicates are averaged, then one line is fitted through those means.

Any protocol replaces the standardised Δ₄₇ of the selected sessions and clears their temperatures, which were calculated from the previous Δ₄₇. T (°C) and T SE show "—" until you run Temperature Conversion again.

How δ¹⁸O is fitted (same as D47crunch): the standards' δ¹⁸O values in the Reference material table are carbonate values. You can enter them on VPDB or on VSMOW (a VPDB value is converted to VSMOW when you save, and the app calculates with the VSMOW value). The instrument measures the CO₂ released by acid digestion, on VSMOW. So each standard's accepted value is first converted to that CO₂ value:

δ¹⁸O CO₂ (VSMOW) = (1000 + δ¹⁸O carbonate (VSMOW)) · α − 1000

α is the calcite acid fractionation factor (Settings → Acid Fractionations) at the session's acid reaction temperature. δ¹⁸O corr is therefore a CO₂ value on VSMOW.

5.1 The δ¹⁸O acid fractionation correction

Carbonates are measured as the CO₂ released when they react with phosphoric acid. That CO₂ is richer in ¹⁸O than the mineral it came from, by an amount that depends on the mineral and on the reaction temperature. The δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionation Correction removes that offset and gives you the δ¹⁸O of the mineral itself.

The result goes into its own column, δ¹⁸O AF. Nothing else changes: δ¹⁸O corr, δ¹³C, Δ₄₇, Δ₄₈ and temperatures stay exactly as they were.

The equation

For each replicate DELTALab computes the acid fractionation factor α of the sample's mineral at its session's reaction temperature:

1000 ln α = a·10⁶/T² + b·10³/T + c

  • T is the session's acid reaction temperature in kelvin (°C + 273.15).
  • a, b and c are the mineral's constants from Settings → Acid Fractionations.

Then it computes the mineral value from the standardised one:

δ¹⁸O AF = (δ¹⁸O corr + 1000) / α − 1000

At 90 °C, calcite gives α ≈ 1.00813: a δ¹⁸O corr of 30.00 ‰ gives a δ¹⁸O AF of about 21.70 ‰.

Before you start: standard δ¹⁸O values are carbonate values

The correction treats δ¹⁸O corr as the δ¹⁸O of the CO₂. Bulk Standardization makes it one: it converts each standard's accepted carbonate value into the CO₂ it releases, using calcite's α at the session's reaction temperature (chapter 5).

The δ¹⁸O values shipped for ETH-1 to ETH-3 in the Standards table are carbonate values on VPDB, as published. If you add your own bulk standards (Settings → Reference material), enter their δ¹⁸O as carbonate values on VPDB too. A CO₂ value there would be shifted by α a second time.

Because the standards go through the same α, a standard whose mineral is set to calcite gets back its own accepted value in δ¹⁸O AF.

Overview of the workflow

  1. Settings → Acid Fractionations: check that every mineral you need is in the list, with the right constants and reference. Done once, not per session.
  2. Sessions Editor: set each session's acid reaction temperature.
  3. Standardization page: run Bulk Standardization.
  4. Standardization page: pick a mineral for each sample on the δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionation Correction card and click Apply.
  5. Read the result box, then check the values: pick δ¹⁸O AF in the Replicates list's δ¹⁸O menu, or look at the Summary table's δ¹⁸O AF column.

Steps 1 and 2 can be done in any order, but both must be done before step 4.

Step 1: Check the minerals in Settings

  2. Type the Mineral name, for example Dolomite.
  3. Type the Reference: the paper the equation comes from. It is required.
  4. Type a, b and c (see the next section). Use 0 for a term the paper's equation doesn't have; a blank field is refused.

Turning a published equation into a, b and c

Papers give the acid fractionation factor in a few different ways. Rewrite the paper's equation so that it reads 1000 ln α = …, then read off:

  • a: the number in front of 10⁶/T²;
  • b: the number in front of 10³/T;
  • c: the number that stands on its own.

Example 1: Das Sharma, Patil & Gopalan (2002), magnesite. The paper's form is

α = exp((4.22 + 6.845·10⁵/T²) / 1000)

  1. Take the natural log of both sides: ln α = (4.22 + 6.845·10⁵/T²) / 1000.
  2. Multiply both sides by 1000: 1000 ln α = 4.22 + 6.845·10⁵/T².
  3. Write 6.845·10⁵/T² as 0.6845 · 10⁶/T².
  4. Read off a = 0.6845, b = 0, c = 4.22.

Example 2: Kim, Mucci & Taylor (2007), calcite. The paper gives 1000 ln α = 3.59 · (10³/T) − 1.79, which already has the right form: a = 0, b = 3.59, c = −1.79.

The same equation is sometimes written as α = exp(3.59/T − 1.79·10⁻³). Multiply the exponent by 1000 and you get 3.59·10³/T − 1.79, so c is still −1.79.

Watch the factor of 1000. It is the most common way to get a constant wrong. c is −1.79, not −0.00179, and b is 3.59, not 3590. A quick check: at 90 °C a carbonate's α should lie roughly between 1.007 and 1.011. The α column in the result box (step 4) shows the value actually used.

  2. Click Apply at the top right of the card.

  • Skipped (only if something was skipped):

    • samples not yet bulk standardized: replicates with no δ¹⁸O corr yet, usually from a session you haven't bulk standardised. They get no δ¹⁸O AF.

  • Replicates (chapter 4): choose δ¹⁸O AF in the menu of the δ¹⁸O column header. Hover over a value to see the δ¹⁸O corr and raw values behind it.
  • Summary table: a δ¹⁸O AF column appears right after δ¹⁸O as soon as any replicate in view has a corrected value. It is the sample mean; a sample never corrected shows "—".

Changing or redoing a correction

Apply always starts from δ¹⁸O corr, never from an earlier δ¹⁸O AF. So you can click it as often as you like: the same minerals and temperatures always give the same result.

  • After changing a sample's mineral, a mineral's constants or a session's temperature: click Apply again. The new values replace the old ones.
  • After re-running Bulk Standardization: δ¹⁸O AF is cleared for the replicates it standardised, because it was computed from the old δ¹⁸O corr. Your minerals are still filled in on the card; click Apply again.
  • Importing new replicates or reprocessing a session also leaves those replicates without a δ¹⁸O AF until you apply again.

Sessions corrected before this version. Earlier versions wrote the correction over δ¹⁸O corr itself. For a session corrected that way, re-run Bulk Standardization once to restore δ¹⁸O corr, then click Apply.

Quick checklist

  • [ ] The minerals you need are in Settings → Acid Fractionations, each with a reference.
  • [ ] Your bulk standards' δ¹⁸O values are carbonate values on VPDB.
  • [ ] Every session has the right acid reaction temperature (default 90 °C).
  • [ ] Bulk Standardization has been run.
  • [ ] Every sample to correct has a mineral on the card.
  • [ ] Apply clicked since the last Bulk Standardization.
  • [ ] The α values in the result box look sensible (≈ 1.007–1.011 at 90 °C).

  3. Check the cards under the list: one for the target, one for each ticked session, and a Result card with the totals.
  4. Click Merge. DELTALab takes a safety backup first.

After the merge a line under the buttons tells you what was done, and the history of the target shows a Merge entry.

Good to know:

  • Only sessions that fit are offered. A session must come from the same instrument, use the same reference gas and belong to the same isotope system as the target. A line under the list says how many other sessions cannot be merged; hover it to see why. If a reference gas has a typo, fix it with Edit first.
  • The target keeps its settings. Every replicate that moves in is recalculated under the target's reference gas and background correction, exactly as with Move. A source that used another background correction gets the target's. If needed, run the Negative Background Correction again (chapter 5).
  • The sources disappear. Their attachments and scan sets come along. Their history stays in the database.
  • Run standardisation again for the merged session. Its old fit does not cover the new replicates.
  • If the sessions overlap in time, the plot is not shaded by session and a note says so.

Deleting replicates or a session

Both are permanent. DELTALab takes a safety backup first; restoring that backup is the only way back (chapter 8).

To leave a replicate out of the calculations but keep it, exclude it instead (chapter 4.1). To set a whole session aside, use Deactivate.

Delete replicates

  1. Tick the replicates.
  2. Click Delete after "Replicates:". The window lists their sample names.
  3. Type DELETE in the box.
  4. Click Delete permanently.

The replicates and their raw acquisitions are removed. The other replicates keep their values and the session gets a red flag, so run standardisation again. Replicates on either side of a deleted one stay separate, even if they have the same sample name. If you delete every replicate, the session disappears from the list.

Delete the session

  1. Clear any ticks and click Delete after "Session:".
  2. Type the session's name exactly as shown.
  3. Click Delete permanently.

This removes the session's replicates and raw acquisitions, its scan sets, its attached files and its Session History.

6.1 Scans and the pressure-baseline correction

You only need this chapter if you use the scan-based method on the Negative Background card. It uses scan sets: 1 to 6 HV scans grouped together. DELTALab fits the m/z 47 (and 48) background against the m/z 44 intensity across them (the pressure-baseline method of Bernasconi et al., 2013).

Where: Sessions Editor → click a session → Edit → Manage scans, at the bottom left of the window. A scan set belongs to one session. The session already knows its instrument, so you are not asked for one.

Steps:

  1. Create a set. Under New set (bottom left), click Choose files and pick 1–6 .scn files. Type a name if you like, then click Create set.
  2. Open the set. Click it in the list on the left. Use the m/z 44 to m/z 49 buttons to see each cup's raw trace.

  3. Set the windows by dragging on the plot. There are no number fields.

    • On-peak window (mass 44): drag across the flat top of the m/z 44 peak. Auto peak makes a first guess; move it to the flat middle.
    • Background windows (mass 47, and mass 48 for Dual Clumped): one or two windows per mass, in the flat baseline on either side of the peak. Auto windows makes a first guess, but always check it. Choosing the baseline is a scientific judgement. Clear left and Clear right remove a window.
    • Masses 45, 46 and 49 are for viewing only.
    • To zoom in on an area of this plot, hold Option (Alt on Windows) and drag a rectangle over it.

    The m/z 47 view. The shaded band left of the peak is a background window; drag on the plot to move it or add one on the right.

  4. Check the fit. Click Fit (next to the mass buttons) to see background against m/z 44 intensity, with the fitted line. Choose Linear or Quadratic for each mass; the plot updates as you choose. Click Save.

  5. Use it. On the Standardization page, pick the set(s) on the Negative Background card and click Apply.

If you change a fit later, it is used the next time the session is processed. You cannot delete a scan set while a session still uses it.

7. Settings

Settings holds the values your whole lab shares: the reference materials, your instruments, the physical constants, the temperature calibrations and the acid fractionation minerals. It also holds a few display choices and the functions for your databases and backups.

The page is one long list of sections. From top to bottom:

  1. Reference material
  2. Mass spectrometers
  3. Computational variables and Temperature calibrations, side by side
  4. δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionations
  5. Display preferences and Additional Functions, side by side

Changing a value here recalculates nothing. A changed value is used the next time you run the step that needs it:

You change It is used by From when
A standard's accepted values Standardization Protocol, Bulk Standardization the next time you run them
A standard's group (Neg. Bkg eval.) the background minimizer the next time you run it
A standard's Show in plots boxes the plots and tables of the Explore page at once
A temperature calibration Temperature Conversion the next time you run it
A mineral's constants δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionation Correction; calcite is also used by Bulk Standardization the next time you run them
A computational variable the calculation of the raw values the next time a session is imported or recalculated
A mass spectrometer's values new sessions sessions you import from now on

The processing steps are described in chapter 5.

Reference material

The table of your standards and their accepted values.

Marked in red: 1 Add standard, 2 the pencils, 3 the Neg. Bkg eval. menus, 4 the Show in plots boxes.

  • Δ₄₇ CDES and Δ₄₈ CDES are the accepted values the Standardization Protocol fits to.
  • δ¹³C VPDB and δ¹⁸O VPDB are the accepted values Bulk Standardization fits to. δ¹⁸O is the carbonate value (chapter 5.1).
  • A value shown as "—" is empty: the standard has no accepted value for that step. The equilibrated gases, for example, have no δ¹³C or δ¹⁸O.

Neg. Bkg eval. (3) puts the standard in a group: Gas group 1–4, Carbonates group 1–4, or "—" for none. The background minimizer assumes all standards in a group share the same true Δ₄₇. The choice is saved as soon as you make it.

Show in plots (4) has one box each for Clumped, Dual and Bulk. The boxes are saved as soon as you tick them.

  • A ticked box means the standard is drawn, and counted as a standard, on that system's Explore page (chapter 4). Bulk is for the δ¹³C and δ¹⁸O tabs of both systems.
  • The boxes do not decide which standards are used in a fit. You choose the standards on the Standardization page.

To edit a standard, click its pencil (2).

  • Change the name, the four values, the Color and the Marker shape, then click Save. Leave a value empty if the standard has none.
  • Delete standard removes it after you confirm.

To add a standard, click Add standard (1). The same window opens, empty. Type a name, fill in the values you have and click Save. Nothing is added until you save, so Cancel leaves the table as it was. A name is required, and a name another standard already uses is refused. The new standard's Show in plots boxes start empty, so tick the systems you use it in.

How a sample name is matched to a standard. A replicate counts as a standard when its sample name is the standard's name:

  • capitals do not matter;
  • a space in the standard's name may also be a hyphen, an underscore or nothing: "ETH 1" matches ETH 1, ETH-1, ETH_1 and eth1;
  • a short replicate label may follow, after a space, hyphen or underscore: a one- or two-digit number, also written with run, rep, r or #, or a single letter. "ETH 1" matches ETH 1 rep2, ETH-1 #3 and ETH 1 a.

Anything else is a sample: ETH 10, ETH 1 dup and My ETH 1 do not match "ETH 1". Name your standards the way they are written in your data. After a standardisation, the result box lists the names that were matched (chapter 5).

Mass spectrometers

Your lab's instruments. They appear in the import window and group the session list. Each instrument has its own δ¹³C ref, δ¹⁸O ref and Acid temp, because each one has its own reference gas tank and acid bath. There is no lab-wide default: every session belongs to an instrument and takes its values from it. The first instrument was created when you first started DELTALab.

  • Add opens a window with the name and the values (the temperature starts at 90 and the others at 0). All of them are required. δ¹⁷O ref is not used for carbonate or CO₂ data, so leave it at 0.
  • A pencil edits the name and the values. A value cannot be left empty.
  • Changed values apply to sessions you import from now on. A session copies the values when it is created and keeps them. To change a session that already exists, use the Sessions Editor (chapter 6).
  • A changed name is changed for the instrument's sessions too.
  • Delete mass spec, in the Edit window, is refused while any session belongs to the instrument. Move those sessions to another instrument in the Sessions Editor first.

Computational variables

The four physical constants used to calculate the raw values: ¹⁷O/¹⁶O (VSMOW), ¹⁸O/¹⁶O (VSMOW), ¹³C/¹²C (VPDB) and λ. They apply to every session.

Click a pencil, type the new value and click Save. Saving recalculates nothing. The new value is used the next time a session is imported or recalculated, so sessions processed before and after the change no longer use the same constants. Change these only if you know why.

Temperature calibrations

The list of Δ→T equations offered by the Temperature Conversion card, beside the computational variables. The Equation column shows each one as DELTALab will use it.

A new install includes three Δ₄₇ calibrations:

  • OGLS23 (Daëron & Vermeesch, 2024)
  • INTERCARBS (Anderson et al., 2021)
  • Fiebig et al. (2024)

They are only a starting set. You can change or delete them. Only Restore Default Settings brings the shipped ones back.

Click Add equation to add one, or a pencil to edit one. Nothing is stored until you click Save. The window asks for a Name and a Model.

Model: Polynomial in 1/T

  • Type the Slope, the 1/T term and the Intercept. The slope is the number in front of 10⁶/T² and the 1/T term is the number in front of 10³/T. For Δ₄₇ = 0.0391×10⁶/T² + 0.154, type 0.0391, 0 and 0.154.
  • Uncertainty says what the paper gives you:
    • None: no calibration uncertainty.
    • Published 1σ per coefficient: type the Slope error and the Intercept error.
    • Full covariance matrix: type the three variances and the three covariances, in the same units as the coefficients. Each pair is typed once.

The choice decides what Propagate calibration uncertainties can do on the Standardization page (chapter 5). Only a full covariance matrix gives a complete temperature uncertainty.

Model: Scaled theoretical curve

  • Type the Scaling factor and the Offset. These are the two fitted numbers.
  • Type the Theoretical curve coefficients, one per power of 1/T, as plain numbers (for 2.391×10⁷, type 23910000 or 2.391e7). + Add term and − Remove last change the number of terms.

Delete calibration, in the Edit window, removes the equation.

Temperature conversion uses Δ₄₇ only.

δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionations

The minerals offered by the δ¹⁸O Acid Fractionation Correction card, each with its reference and its a, b and c constants. Chapter 5.1 explains how to turn a published equation into these three numbers.

  • Calcite, aragonite and magnesite are included.
  • Add creates a new mineral; a pencil edits one. Type 0 for a term the paper's equation does not have.
  • Delete mineral, in the Edit window, removes it. Samples that were assigned to it go back to "—" on the Standardization page.
  • Every mineral needs a reference. One shown as Missing was carried over from an older version; edit it to add one.
  • Calcite is also used by Bulk Standardization, to convert the standards' δ¹⁸O (chapter 5).

Display preferences

  • Marker size: the size of the points in every plot, from 2 to 12.
  • Default environment: which isotope system DELTALab starts on: Clumped Isotopes or Dual Clumped. Changing it does not switch the page you are on.

Additional Functions

Your databases. DELTALab works on one database at a time.

  • New database creates an empty database. Choose where to save it, with Browse… or by pasting a full path, give it a Name and click Create. It never overwrites a file that already exists.
  • Open database switches to another DELTALab database file. Choose it with Browse…, or paste its full path, and click Open. A file that is not a DELTALab database is refused.
  • Rename database changes the name shown in the status bar and on its backups. The file on disk keeps its name.

After New database or Open database the app reloads on the chosen database, and DELTALab starts on it next time too.

Each database has its own sessions, Session History, attached files and reference material table. A new one starts with the shipped reference materials. The other settings on this page are shared by all your databases.

Backups.

  • Back up now makes a backup at any moment.
  • Restore backup opens the list of this database's backups.
  • Export current saves a copy of the database you are using right now.

Chapter 8 explains when backups are made automatically, what an export contains and what a restore does.

Restore Default Settings resets the Reference material table, the computational variables and the temperature calibrations to the shipped values. Standards and calibrations you added or edited are lost.

  • DELTALab asks you to confirm first and takes a safety backup, so you can undo it with Restore backup.
  • It does not change mass spectrometers (or their reference gas and acid temperature), acid fractionation minerals, marker size, the default environment or your backups.
  • It does not recalculate your sessions.

8. Backups, restore and updates

DELTALab keeps all your work in two files on your computer: the database (sessions, raw acquisitions, results, Session History, attached files, reference material table) and the settings file Storage.json (calibrations, minerals, constants, instruments, display choices). A backup is a copy of both, taken together.

Where your data is

Both files start in the DELTALab data folder:

  • macOS: ~/Library/Application Support/DELTALab/
  • Windows: %APPDATA%\DELTALab\

If you made or opened another database (chapter 7), it stays where you put it. Hover over db: in the status bar to see its full path. Storage.json stays in the data folder.

The backups are in a backups folder next to the database file, one folder per backup. A backup's folder is named after its date and time, for example 20261005-154214, and holds database.zip and Storage.json.

Updating or reinstalling DELTALab does not touch the data folder.

Automatic backups

DELTALab makes a backup by itself:

  • once a day, the first time you start the app that day. If an ordinary backup from today already exists, none is added;
  • the first time you start a new version. This backup carries a label, <old version> → <new version>, and is your way back if an update goes wrong;
  • the first time you open a database that day, with Open database;
  • just before an action that removes or reshapes data:
    • deleting a session;
    • deleting replicates;
    • renaming the sample of replicates;
    • splitting a session;
    • importing an Easotope database;
    • Restore Default Settings;
    • restoring a backup.

For each database, DELTALab keeps the newest 5 ordinary backups and, separately, the newest 3 labelled ones. A busy day of deletions can never push out an update backup, and the other way round.

Backups and restores are not written to Session History (chapter 4.3).

Making a backup or a copy

Both buttons are in Settings → Additional Functions (chapter 7).

  • Back up now makes a backup at any moment, for example before a risky change. It is an ordinary backup: it is removed once five newer ones exist.
  • Export current saves a copy of the database and settings you are using right now, as a file named DELTALab-database-<date and time>.zip. It holds database.db and Storage.json.

Keep an export anywhere you like: an external drive, a shared folder, or send it to a colleague. An export is never removed by DELTALab, unlike backups. Export a copy whenever you want to keep a state for good.

Restoring a backup

  1. Go to Settings and click Restore backup. The window lists the backups of the open database, newest first, with their date and size.
  2. Click Restore on the backup you want.
  3. Confirm. DELTALab reloads.

What the list can tell you:

  • A label such as 1.0.0 → 1.0.1 marks the backup taken before an update.
  • was "…" means the database had another name when the backup was made.
  • database not recorded marks a backup too old to say which database it belongs to. Make sure it is the right one before you restore it; DELTALab asks you to check.
  • A line at the bottom says how many backups of other databases in the same folder are not shown. Open that database to restore them.

What happens:

  • The backup is checked first. A damaged backup is refused and nothing is changed.
  • A safety backup of the current state is made, so a restore can always be undone. It appears at the top of the list.
  • The database and the settings are replaced by the backup's. Everything you did after that backup is gone from the app: imports, processing, edits and changed settings. If you work with several databases, remember that the settings are shared by all of them.

To undo a restore, restore the safety backup that was made just before it.

Using an exported copy

Use this to bring an exported copy back, or to move your data to another computer.

  1. Quit DELTALab.
  2. Unzip the export.
  3. Put its database.db and Storage.json in the data folder, in place of the two files there. Keep the old ones somewhere else first if you may need them.
  4. Start DELTALab.

Use a copy made with the same version of DELTALab, or an older one.

To look at an exported database without replacing anything, unzip it and use Open database. That keeps your current settings.

To look inside a backup without DELTALab: double-click its database.zip to unzip it. The database.db inside is an SQLite database. Very old backups may hold a plain database.db instead of a zip; both kinds restore correctly.

Updates

DELTALab checks for a new version each time it starts. When there is one, a banner appears under the top bar.

Marked in red: 1 the new version, 2 Update & Restart, 3 close the banner.

  • Update & Restart (2) downloads the new version, installs it and restarts DELTALab. The banner shows "Downloading…" with a percentage while it works.
  • × (3) closes the banner if you want to update later. It comes back the next time you start DELTALab.
  • If your app is up to date, you see nothing.
  • If the check or the installation fails, the banner says so and shows the error.
  • On a computer DELTALab is not built for, the banner says that automatic updates are not available. Close it with ×.

Your data is not changed by an update. The first time the new version starts, it makes the labelled backup described above.

If an update goes wrong, go back to the version you had:

  1. Restore the backup labelled with the version you came from (Settings → Restore backup).
  2. Quit DELTALab.
  3. Install that older version again. It is the one on the left of the arrow in the label.

Your data is then exactly as it was before the update. Anything you did in the new version is not in it; it is in the safety backup the restore made.

DELTALab is built for Macs with Apple Silicon and for 64-bit Windows. Only some versions are also built for Intel Macs, and none for Windows on ARM. When the newest version was not built for your computer, there is nothing to update to and DELTALab says so.
